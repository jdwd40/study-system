# Purpose and Reader

## Objective

Identify a specific reader and purpose, then write and revise a 120–180 word piece to make the intended message clear.

## Content

### Why this matters
A piece can be grammatically correct and still fail because it answers the wrong question or assumes knowledge the reader does not have. Begin with the intended effect, not impressive wording.

### Core explanation
Before drafting, answer three questions: Who is this for? What should they understand, feel or do? What do they already know? Write a brief: “For [reader], this piece will [purpose], so that [desired response].” Select details that serve that brief. Tone is the relationship you establish with the reader; it is not a rule that all writing must sound formal.

Planning chooses the message and relevant details. Drafting puts ideas into words. Revision checks whether those words achieve the purpose. Editing improves sentences; proofreading catches remaining surface errors. These activities can overlap, but fixing every comma while drafting can prevent you from discovering what you mean.

### Worked example
Vague brief: “Write about backups.” Specific brief: “For a colleague who does not manage servers, explain why a backup must be tested, so that they agree to a restore check.”

Before: “In the context of operational resilience, the implementation of comprehensive backup validation processes is imperative.”
After: “A backup is useful only if we can restore it. Let’s restore one sample file this week to check that our backups work.”
The revision names the practical issue and the requested action. It fits this reader and purpose; it is not a universal instruction to make all writing short or technical.

### Short exercise
Choose a real topic: an everyday explanation, a small technical idea, a historical claim, or an introduction to a fictional place. Write the one-sentence brief, then a 120–180 word baseline draft. Reread it as the intended reader. Revise three sentences or details to improve relevance or clarity; explain one change. A fiction brief may aim for curiosity or atmosphere rather than an action.

### Connections
Apply the same reader awareness to a Software Systems Engineering explanation, a sourced history paragraph, or a Dark Frontier scene. Existing material is optional; do not rewrite the manuscript as part of this lesson.

### Short recap
Clear writing serves a particular reader and purpose. A brief guides selection. Draft first, then check the effect; sentence correctness alone does not establish success.

### Check questions
1. Who is your reader, and what do they already know?
2. What should they understand, feel or do after reading?
3. Which detail in your draft directly serves that purpose?
4. What did you change, and why should it help the reader?

### Next step
Save the baseline and revision for later comparison. Continue with Generating and Selecting Ideas when time and energy allow. No lesson completion, mastery or study time is recorded merely by reading this plan.

## Examples

- Rewrite a backup request for a non-specialist colleague.
- Adapt a description of a fictional place to create curiosity rather than explain its whole history.

## Takeaways

- Name the reader and intended effect before drafting.
- Select details by relevance, not by how much you know.
- Judge revision against the brief, not a universal preference for formality or brevity.

## Flashcards

- Q: What three questions should guide a writing brief?
  A: Who is the reader, what is the purpose or intended effect, and what does the reader already know?
- Q: How does revision differ from proofreading?
  A: Revision improves meaning, structure and reader effect; proofreading catches remaining surface errors.

## Revision Questions

- How do purpose and audience change what details you include?
- What is the difference between drafting, revision, editing and proofreading?
- Show one revision and explain how it serves your reader.
